Sinking Fund Planner

Keep this separate. Not in checking, not mixed with your emergency fund. Label the account “Planned Expenses” and automate the transfer on payday. The whole point is that the money is already there when the bill arrives.

How the maths works

Monthly set-aside = Sum of all known annual expenses ÷ 12

How to use it

  1. List every expense that arrives once or twice a year: car registration, insurance premiums, holiday gifts, annual subscriptions, vehicle maintenance, school costs, pet vaccinations.
  2. Enter the full annual cost of each, not the monthly share — the calculator divides.
  3. Move the resulting monthly figure to a separate account on payday, automatically.

A worked example

Car registration $180, holiday gifts $600, car maintenance $480, subscriptions $200 and an insurance premium $240 total $1,700 a year — $142 a month. None of those five are surprises. They only feel like emergencies because nothing was set aside, and each one lands on a credit card at 22% APR instead.

Common questions

What is a sinking fund?

Money set aside monthly for an expense you know is coming but that does not arrive monthly. It is not an emergency fund — an emergency fund covers the things you cannot predict, while a sinking fund covers the things you can. Keeping them separate is what stops predictable bills from draining the cushion meant for real emergencies.

Where should I keep sinking fund money?

In its own account, labelled for the purpose, separate from both checking and your emergency fund. The label matters more than it sounds: money in an account called "Planned Expenses" gets spent on planned expenses, and money sitting in checking gets spent on whatever happens next.

How is this different from just budgeting?

A monthly budget only sees monthly bills, so an annual $600 expense is invisible for eleven months and then breaks the budget in the twelfth. A sinking fund converts it into a $50 monthly line that your budget can actually see and plan around.

What this calculator is not

It is an educational model, not a projection and certainly not advice. It knows nothing about your income, your state, your debts or your benefits status, and it ignores taxes and fees unless the page says otherwise. If you receive SSI or SSDI some of this maths works differently and getting it wrong can cost you eligibility — start with the Disability Wealth Guide instead.
