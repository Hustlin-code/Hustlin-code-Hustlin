Sinking Fund

Money set aside every month for an expense you already know is coming — the plates, the tires, December. It is the opposite of an emergency fund, and keeping the two apart is the whole point.

Also called: irregular expense fund · planned savings fund · Life Just Happened Fund · savings bucket · bill-smoothing fund

Reviewed 12 August 2026 · Sourced from the SEC's disclosure rules, the 18th-century statutes the term came from, and BLS spending data — with the personal-finance parts labeled as convention

The short version

A sinking fund is money you set aside every month for an expense you already know is coming, so that when the bill lands the money is already there. The arithmetic is one line: the total cost divided by the number of months until it is due.

It exists because a monthly budget can only see monthly bills. A $600 December is invisible for eleven months and then breaks the budget in the twelfth — not because the money was never available, but because it was never separated. A sinking fund converts an annual shock into a monthly line item. That is all it does. It does not create money, and if there is nothing left over at the end of the month there is nothing to divide.

Key takeaways
  • Sinking fund = an expense you know is coming. Emergency fund = one you don't. Car registration is a sinking fund. A transmission failing is an emergency. New tires are a sinking fund, because you can see the tread.
  • The formula is total cost ÷ months until due. A $175 renewal seven months out is $25 a month. Our Life Just Happened Fund calculator runs the annual case, dividing your yearly total by 12.
  • The term is borrowed, and the thing it was borrowed from is real. A sinking fund provision on a bond requires the issuer to set money aside on a schedule to retire debt instead of facing the whole principal at maturity — a disclosure the SEC requires under 17 CFR § 229.202(b)(1).
  • The personal-finance version is a convention, not a rule. No statute, no regulator, no required formula, no official list of categories. Every dollar figure on this page is either sourced and named, or labeled as our own illustration.
  • Year one costs more than year two. Starting funds that are already four months from due date means short runways. The six-fund example below runs $378 a month in the first year and about $260 a month once each cycle resets to its full length.
  • The interest is not the reason. Saving $56 a month for ten months in a 4.00% APY account earns about $8.48. Charging the same $560 at 22.15% and paying $56 a month costs about $64.84 in interest. The gap is in the borrowing, not the yield.
  • It only works if there is margin. If the month already ends at zero, a sinking fund has nothing to draw from. That is a budget and income problem first, and this page says so plainly rather than pretending otherwise.

What a sinking fund actually is

The renewal notice for the plates arrives in March. It is $175. It was $175 last March, and the March before that. In March there is $58 in checking and four days until payday, so the $175 goes on a credit card, and by the time it is paid off it cost more than $175.

Two months later the tires finally go. You knew they were going — you could see the tread, you have been meaning to deal with it since winter. That goes on the card too.

Neither of those was an emergency. Both of them were on the calendar. What was missing was not money and not discipline; it was a place to put the money in advance.

A sinking fund is that place. You take an expense you know is coming, divide its cost by the number of months until it arrives, and move that much every month into savings set aside for it. When the bill lands, the money is already there. That is the entire mechanism, and there is nothing clever in it.

The one-sentence version

A sinking fund pays for the bill you can see coming. An emergency fund pays for the one you can't.

What makes it useful is not the saving. It is the visibility. A monthly budget is a list of monthly numbers, and an expense that arrives once a year does not appear on it. For eleven months the budget looks balanced and is not. In the twelfth month the budget is wrong by $600 and it feels like bad luck.

Why we call it a Life Just Happened Fund

“Sinking fund” is the textbook name and it is a terrible one. It sounds like something going under, which is the opposite of what it does. On this site the tool is called the Life Just Happened Fund calculator, because that is the moment you reach for it — the vet, the tire, the school trip, the December that arrives on schedule every year and surprises everybody anyway. Same mechanism, honest name. We keep the term “sinking fund” on this page because it is what the rest of the world calls it and you will meet it that way.

Where the term came from, and why that matters here

This is worth two minutes, because it is the only part of the page with a documented rule behind it.

In bond finance, a sinking fund provision obliges the issuer to set money aside on a schedule — or retire portions of the debt early — so that the principal does not arrive as one lump at maturity. It is not a soft practice. When a company registers debt securities with the SEC, Item 202 of Regulation S-K requires it to describe the “[p]rovisions with respect to maturity, interest, conversion, redemption, amortization, sinking fund, or retirement” (17 CFR § 229.202(b)(1)). On a municipal issue the same idea shows up as a schedule of mandatory redemptions: fixed amounts of the bond are called in on fixed dates rather than all at the end.

The idea is older than either market, and it started with governments trying to pay down war debt. Britain's National Debt Reduction Act 1786 (26 Geo. 3 c. 31) is titled, in full, “An Act for vesting certain Sums in Commissioners at the end of every Quarter of a Year, to be by them applied to the Reduction of the National Debt.” A fixed sum, every quarter, handed to people whose only job was to retire debt with it. The commissioners created by that act still exist; the UK Debt Management Office notes that little remains of their original function, “the full remnants of the earlier sinking fund concept having been removed by the National Loans Act 1968.”

The United States copied the design almost immediately. The Act of August 12, 1790 (ch. 47, 1 Stat. 186), “An Act making Provision for the Reduction of the Public Debt,” directed surplus revenue to “the purchase of the debt of the United States, at its market price,” under commissioners who were the President of the Senate, the Chief Justice, the Secretary of State, the Secretary of the Treasury and the Attorney General. Contemporaries called it the sinking fund.

Watch this

Everything above is documented. Everything below is an analogy. There is no statute, no regulator, no licensed product and no required formula for a personal sinking fund. Nobody defines the categories, nobody sets the amounts, and no agency publishes a recommended number of funds. The rest of this page is arithmetic and convention, and it is labeled as such in the ledger below.

Sinking fund or emergency fund: the line between them

This is the distinction the term exists for, and it is the one people collapse. An emergency fund covers what you could not have predicted. A sinking fund covers what you could. Run one pot for both and the predictable expense eats the cushion you were holding for the unpredictable one — and then the unpredictable one arrives anyway.

The test is not how much it hurts. It is whether you could have written down a date and a price a year ago.

The expenseWhich fundWhy

The tires row is the reason this page exists. Tires are the most predictable large car expense there is, and they get treated as a shock in the moment they fail, which is how they end up on a credit card. The average rate charged on card accounts assessed interest was 22.15% in May 2026 (Federal Reserve G.19). A $560 balance also raises your credit utilization, so a foreseeable purchase quietly costs you interest and score at the same time.

The blurry middle

Some expenses are known in kind but not in date — a water heater at year 11, a roof at year 22 of a 25-year life. Those sit between the two. The workable treatment is to fund them like a sinking fund with a deliberately wide range, and accept that the emergency fund is the backstop if the date arrives early. Nothing official governs this; it is a judgment call and we are labeling it as one.

The arithmetic, and the multi-goal version

One expense, one line:

Total cost ÷ months until it's due = monthly set-aside

When the expense is annual, months until due is 12, and the formula collapses into the one the Life Just Happened Fund calculator uses: add up everything you know arrives once a year, divide by twelve, move that much on payday.

Worked example

A set of four tires will cost about $560, and the tread says June. It is August, so that is 10 months.

$560 ÷ 10 = $56 a month. After five months the balance is $280. In June it is $560, you pay cash, and the fund goes back to zero.

The alternative, priced out: charge the $560 in June at 22.15% and pay $56 a month. It takes 12 payments, not 10, and totals $624.84 — $64.84 of interest for the privilege of not having decided in August.

Same $56 a month. One version ends in June with tires and no debt. The other ends the following June having paid $64.84 extra.

Several funds, one account

Almost nobody has one of these. You have six, they are all running at once, and they live in a single savings account tracked as separate balances on paper. Here is a plausible set, priced in August 2026. Only one figure is sourced; the rest are our own illustration.

Year one is the expensive year

$378 a month is a real number and for many readers it is an impossible one. It is also temporary, and the reason is in the Months column: four of those six funds have short runways because you started late, not because the expense is large. Once each cycle resets to its full length — gifts and registration over 12 months, insurance over its 6, a set of tires over the roughly 36 months it lasts — the same list settles at about $260 a month. Our own arithmetic, and the single most discouraging-then-encouraging fact about starting one of these.

What actually goes in one, and what the real numbers are

There is no official list. What follows is the set of expenses that show up most often, with a plain marker on which figures are sourced.

Sourced. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' Consumer Expenditure Survey is the closest thing to a government answer for “what does this cost.” For 2024, average annual expenditures per consumer unit were $78,535, of which transportation was $13,318 and “other vehicle expenses” — the bucket holding maintenance, repairs, insurance, registration and finance charges — was $4,206 (Consumer Expenditures — 2024). Inside that, vehicle maintenance and repairs averaged $984 and vehicle insurance averaged $1,993.

Read those honestly. They are averages across all consumer units, including households with no car at all, which drags the averages down for anyone who has one. They are a starting point for a guess, not your number. Your registration renewal and your own premium notice are better data than any national average, and you already have both on paper.

The usual list

  • Vehicle — registration, inspection or emissions, insurance premium, tires, brakes, scheduled maintenance
  • Home — property tax and homeowners insurance if they are not escrowed, HVAC service, appliance replacement, the deductible you would owe on a claim
  • Annual bills — subscriptions, professional license or certification renewals, union or association dues, tax preparation
  • People and pets — December, birthdays, school fees and supplies, sports and activity fees, vaccinations and annual vet visits
  • Body — dental work, glasses or contacts, the medical deductible that resets every January

The figure to be careful with

You will see “budget 1% to 2% of your home's value per year for maintenance” quoted everywhere as though it were a standard. It is a convention with no agency behind it. No federal body publishes that range. It is a reasonable planning placeholder and a bad thing to treat as authority, and the same is true of every “10% of income” and “three months of expenses” figure in this corner of the internet. Where this page can name a source it does. Where it can't, it says so.

Where the money lives and how it gets tracked

The arithmetic is the easy half. The half that decides whether this survives contact with a hard month is plumbing.

One account or many

The two workable structures, and the trade-off between them:

One account, tracked on paperA separate account per fund
SetupOne transfer, one spreadsheetSix sub-accounts to open and name
BalancesYou have to maintain the split yourselfThe bank maintains it for you
Failure modeThe spreadsheet stops getting updated and the balance becomes a mysterySix accounts becomes admin, and admin gets abandoned
Raiding itEasy, and invisible until you reconcileRequires a deliberate act you will remember

Neither is correct. Which one survives depends on whether you are more likely to abandon a spreadsheet or a set of accounts, and you probably already know which of those you are.

What a high-yield account adds, precisely

Keeping it in a savings account that pays a real APY costs nothing and is worth having. It is not the reason to do this. Run the tire fund at 4.00% APY — $56 a month for ten months — and you finish with $568.48. The yield contributed $8.48. At 0.50%, closer to what a large branch bank pays on basic savings, it contributes about $1.05. Compare that to the $64.84 in interest the credit-card version cost, and the point is clear: the money in this technique comes from not borrowing, not from earning. If you want to see what a yield does over years rather than months, that is compound interest and a different time scale entirely.

Two practical notes. Keep it somewhere you cannot reach with a debit card, because friction is the feature. And deposits at an insured bank are covered up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category (12 U.S.C. § 1821(a)(1)(E)) — not a constraint most sinking funds will test, but the reason a savings account is the right shape of container and an investment account is not. Money needed in eleven months should not be exposed to a market that can be down in eleven months.

Spending it, and restarting the clock

Spending a sinking fund is not a failure. It is the fund working. The step people skip is the one after: the balance is now zero, the next occurrence has a new date, and the monthly number has to be recalculated from that date. Tires bought in June 2027 that last three years means $560 ÷ 36, or about $16 a month, starting in July. Nobody does this, which is why the second set of tires is as much of a crisis as the first.

Raiding one fund to cover another is the real leak, and it does not announce itself. Taking $200 out of the insurance fund in October to cover December means the February premium is $200 short and you find out in February. If you move money between funds, write it down in the same place you track the balances, so the shortfall shows up in October when there is still time to do something about it.

The honest limit: it needs margin to work

A sinking fund is a scheduling tool. It moves money from a month you have it to a month you need it. It does not manufacture any, and the technique is useless — not hard, useless — when the month already ends at zero.

That is the situation a lot of readers of this site are in, and the usual advice at this point is to try harder. That is not advice, it is a scolding. Here is the actual order of operations when there is nothing to divide.

  1. Find out where it goes. Not to feel bad about it — to see whether a gap exists at all. The budget calculator takes income in and known bills out and tells you the number.
  2. Match the money to the paycheck. If income arrives weekly or twice a month and bills arrive monthly, the shortfall can be timing rather than volume. The paycheck split calculator divides bills across the checks that actually land before them.
  3. Stabilize before you optimize. Stage 1 · Survive covers the next 30 days — banking, income, which bills genuinely have to be paid first. Stage 2 · Stabilize is where bills, debt triage and a first cushion live. Both are free and need no account.
  4. Then start with one fund and one number. Not six. The smallest predictable expense you can name, funded at $10 or $20 a month. The point of the first fund is not the money, it is finding out the account still has a balance in it next month.

There is also a sequencing question with no correct answer: whether to fund these before or alongside high-rate debt payoff. Paying an extra $100 against a 22.15% balance beats earning 4.00% on $100 in savings on the arithmetic alone. But a reader with zero set aside puts the next unavoidable expense straight back on the card, which undoes the payoff and adds a new balance. Both positions are defensible. We are laying out the trade-off and stopping, because which side you land on depends on facts about your life that a glossary page does not have.

What trips people up

  • Running it out of the same account as the emergency fund. Then the registration renewal eats the cushion, and the actual emergency arrives to an empty account. Two purposes, two balances, even if it is one account and a spreadsheet.
  • Filing tires, brakes and batteries as emergencies. They are the most predictable large car expenses there are. Misfiling them is the single most common route to a card balance at 22.15%.
  • Never restarting the clock. The fund hits zero when you spend it, and the next occurrence has a new date. If the monthly number is not recalculated from that date, the second one is as bad as the first.
  • Starting six funds at once. The month-one total is the highest it will ever be, because every runway is short. It reads as proof the technique is unaffordable, when it is only proof that you started late. Start with one.
  • Quiet borrowing between funds. Moving $200 from insurance to cover December means finding out in February. If you do it, write it down where you keep the balances.
  • Chasing yield instead of the point. Spending a weekend comparing savings rates to gain a few dollars over ten months, while a foreseeable expense sits on a card. Get the money out of the borrowing first; the APY is a rounding error at these balances and time horizons.
  • Investing money you need in eleven months. A sinking fund has a date. An account that can be down 15% on that date is the wrong container, however good the long-run average is.
  • Treating the number as a verdict. $378 a month is arithmetic about your car and your December, not a judgment about you. If the number is impossible, the useful response is a budget and an income conversation, not abandoning the idea.

Frequently asked questions

What is a sinking fund?

A sinking fund is money set aside on a schedule to pay for an expense you already know is coming, so the cost is spread across the months before it arrives instead of landing all at once. The arithmetic is the total cost divided by the number of months until it is due: a $175 registration renewal seven months out is $25 a month. The term is borrowed from bond finance, where a sinking fund provision requires an issuer to provide for retiring debt on a schedule rather than facing the whole principal at maturity. In personal finance it is a budgeting convention, not a regulated product.

What is the difference between a sinking fund and an emergency fund?

A sinking fund is for an expense you know is coming. An emergency fund is for one you don't. Car registration, an insurance premium and new tires are sinking funds, because you can put a date and a price on all three. A transmission failing, a job ending and an emergency room visit are emergencies. The test is not how much it hurts but whether you could have written down the date and the amount a year in advance. Keeping them in one pot means the predictable expense drains the cushion held for the unpredictable one.

How do you calculate a sinking fund payment?

Divide the total cost by the number of months until the money is needed. A $560 set of tires needed in ten months is $56 a month. When the expense is annual, months until due is 12, which is the case the Life Just Happened Fund calculator on this site runs: add up everything that arrives once a year and divide by twelve. With several funds running at once, calculate each one separately and add the monthly figures together. The first year is the expensive one, because the runways are short.

Why is it called a sinking fund?

The name comes from government debt, not household budgeting. Britain's National Debt Reduction Act 1786 vested a fixed sum each quarter in commissioners to be applied to reducing the national debt, and the United States copied the design in the Act of August 12, 1790, which directed surplus revenue to the purchase of the public debt at market price. Debt being retired was said to be sunk. The name is unintuitive for personal use, which is why this site calls the tool a Life Just Happened Fund instead.

Do sinking funds need separate bank accounts?

No. The two workable structures are one savings account with the balances tracked separately on paper or in a spreadsheet, and a separate sub-account per fund. Separate accounts mean the bank maintains the split and raiding a fund takes a deliberate act you will remember. One account is less admin but the tracking is entirely on you, and an out-of-date spreadsheet turns the balance into a mystery. Neither is correct in general. The one that works is the one you will not abandon.

Should a sinking fund be in a high-yield savings account?

A savings account paying a real APY costs nothing and is a sensible container, but the yield is not where the value is. Saving $56 a month for ten months at 4.00% APY finishes at $568.48, so the interest added $8.48. Charging the same $560 at the 22.15% average rate on card accounts assessed interest and paying $56 a month costs $64.84 in interest and takes twelve payments instead of ten. The money comes from not borrowing. Money needed within a year does not belong in an investment account, because the date will not wait for a recovery.

What happens if you spend your sinking fund on something else?

The expense it was funding still arrives, and it arrives short. The leak is quiet because the shortfall shows up months later: move $200 out of the insurance fund in October and you discover it in February when the premium is due. If money moves between funds, record it in the same place the balances live so the gap is visible while there is still time to close it. After any fund is spent, the balance is zero and the monthly contribution has to be recalculated from the next due date.
