Opening a Bank Account After Prison

Your record is not what stops you. Missing documents and the wrong branch are. Here is the order to do this in, what to bring, and what to walk away from.

Published 1 August 2026 · 9 min read

Start with the thing almost nobody says out loud: banks do not run criminal background checks to open a checking account. Your conviction is not on the application and it is not what gets you declined.

What banks screen is ChexSystems — a reporting agency that records banking history. Unpaid overdrafts. Accounts closed for cause. Suspected fraud. If you have never held an account, or you closed yours in good standing before you went in, there is likely nothing in your file at all.

That matters because the fear of being turned down keeps people out of banks for years, and every one of those years is expensive. Cashing one $500 cheque a week at a check-cashing store at 3% costs about $780 a year — money that buys nothing. Meanwhile no direct deposit, no way to build credit, no safe place for what you do have.

The real obstacles are documents and address. Both are solvable, in this order.

Step 1 — Replace your ID first

This is the step that decides how fast the rest goes, and it is the one most often skipped. Nearly every institution requires one primary photo ID plus a second proof of identity. Usually that means a state ID or driver's licence, plus a Social Security card or birth certificate. A release document on its own is generally not enough.

If your ID expired inside — most do — work in this order:

  1. Birth certificate from the vital records office in the state you were born. Everything else is easier to get once you have this.
  2. Social Security card replacement, free from the Social Security Administration. You can often start this online.
  3. State ID at the DMV, using the two above.

Ask about the fee waiver. Many states have a reduced-fee or fee-waived ID process for people released from custody, and some issue an ID before release. It is rarely advertised. Ask the DMV directly, and ask your reentry programme or parole officer — a lot of them have a budget line for exactly this and will pay it.

Call 211 if you are stuck. It is a free national line that connects you to local reentry and assistance programmes, and most people have never heard of it.

Step 2 — Sort out the address question before you walk in

Banks need an address on file. If you are in a halfway house, a shelter, or staying with family, you are not disqualified — you just need the right paperwork.

What is commonly accepted:

  • A letter on letterhead from the halfway house, shelter, or reentry programme confirming you reside there
  • Written confirmation from a parole or probation officer
  • A letter from the person whose address you are using, sometimes with one of their utility bills

Phone the branch first and ask what they accept. Thirty seconds on the phone saves a wasted trip — and more importantly it avoids a declined application, which can itself land in your ChexSystems file and make the next attempt harder.

Step 3 — Pick the right institution, and ask for the right product

Where you apply matters more than anything about you. Three routes work, roughly in order of how well they tend to go:

Local credit unions

Usually the best option and the one most people skip. Credit unions are member-owned rather than shareholder-owned, decisions are frequently made by a person rather than an algorithm, and many run second-chance programmes specifically for people rebuilding. Ask directly whether they have experience with returning citizens — plenty do, and the ones that do are used to the paperwork.

Online banks that skip ChexSystems

Chime, Varo and GO2bank generally do not screen through ChexSystems, and you can apply from a phone. These are real FDIC-insured accounts with debit cards, not prepaid cards. The trade-off is no branch to walk into when something goes wrong.

Second-chance checking at a larger bank

Most big banks have a designated second-chance product that converts to standard checking after roughly a year of good standing. Ask for it by name. Applying for standard checking and being declined puts an inquiry on your file for no reason.

Bank On is worth ten minutes. It is a national initiative certifying accounts that meet minimum standards: no overdraft fees, low or no minimum balance, no hidden charges. Certified accounts exist at institutions in most metro areas, and the standards were written precisely to stop banks charging the most to the people with the least.

What to walk away from

Three products will be marketed at you hard in your first months out. All three are designed for exactly your situation, and none of them are on your side.

Prepaid debit cards. They feel like bank accounts and are not. Monthly fees, reload fees, ATM fees — and critically, they build no banking history and no credit. A year on a prepaid card leaves you exactly where you started.

Check-cashing stores. Typically 1–4% of every cheque. At $500 a week that is roughly $780 a year for a service a bank account provides free.

Payday loans. Around $300 borrowed costs $345–400 in two weeks — an annual rate near 400%. The average borrower takes eight loans a year, because the first one makes the next one necessary. If you are close to needing one, call 211 first.

The first week after approval

Getting the account open is the milestone. These four things make it stick:

  1. Set up direct deposit, even partial. It is the single strongest signal of a stable account, and many banks waive fees once it is running.
  2. Turn on every alert in the mobile app. Overdrafts are what put people into ChexSystems in the first place, and an alert catches one before the fee does.
  3. Decline overdraft "protection" on debit purchases. It is not protection. It lets a $4 coffee become a $39 charge. Declined at the register is annoying; a $39 fee is worse.
  4. Open a second, separate savings account and move $25 into it. That is the beginning of the buffer that means you never need a payday loan.

Your record is not the obstacle

Worth repeating, because the assumption costs people years: your conviction is not on the application. What the bank looks at is banking history, address, and identity documents. All three are things you can produce.

The system is not built to make this easy. It is not built to make it impossible either, and the difference matters — because if you believe it is impossible, you stop trying, and the fees you pay instead compound just as quietly as savings would have.
